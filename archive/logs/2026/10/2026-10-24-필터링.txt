On Friday, OpenAI said that it will train its models using cached copies and simulated versions of the internet instead of the live web because the models are too unpredictable to be trusted with live internet access.
The frontier lab announced its new policy two weeks after its rival, Anthropic, began limiting its newest and, in theory, most capable models from accessing the live internet. Researchers adopted these policies after months of incidents in which models pursuing training tasks broke into external systems in search of answers, collaborated on obscure message boards, and developed exotic hacking strategies.
“Based on the current state of alignment research, monitoring systems, and the security of internet infrastructure, we believe the responsible approach is to conduct frontier model training with no internet access,” the company said in a blog post.
OpenAI said that as recently as September 20, one of its frontier models exploited a misconfiguration to access the internet. That led the company to pause training work for the rest of the month so it could develop and test new approaches to securing its models.
The decision to train these models in sandboxes cut off from the internet is notable because employees at frontier labs have insisted that doing so would be nearly infeasible.
Keeping AI data centers separate from the internet “would need research teams to essentially be embedded inside of data centers in Iowa or wherever else around the world,” former OpenAI researcher Steven Adler told Fortune this month. “It’s a lot of overhead, and it isn’t clear to me that the safety benefits of it are large enough to merit just how large a tax it would be for a company to do it unilaterally.”
“In nearly every post I make on X, without fail, I get between five and ten comments about my inability to secure a sandbox, or asking why the hell we can’t just unplug the model from the internet,” an anonymous security engineer at OpenAI wrote in a September post on X. “These comments reflect a widening gap between where a lot of organizations and people are today and what the frontier labs are dealing with. There is also a general misunderstanding of how model training and evaluation work at these labs.”
The engineer, whose role has been confirmed previously by OpenAI, described how complex the training runs launched by OpenAI engineers are, noting that they require access a variety of resources and tools across many domains, and that thousands of researchers build, modify and test the environments. “So is it as easy as just putting it in a sandbox? No, it’s not,” he concluded.
Now, OpenAI says that “we must always be able to prevent a frontier model from achieving unauthorized, arbitrary access to the live internet.” That comes after the company deployed swarms of agents in red-teaming exercises that attempted to escape from their sandbox. That effort revealed a range of vulnerabilities that convinced the lab to adopt its new approach.
To meet that requirement while still reaching its training goals, the lab said it created a “a vast set of indexed and cached web content with which our models can interact, without risk of affecting external systems.”
Whether that strategy will perform as well as letting models run freely on the open internet remains to be seen, but it is noteworthy that both frontier labs have determined their most powerful technology can’t be left unsupervised on the digital commons.

Three-week on-site audit at OpenAI reveals major gaps in AI safety monitoring
Two researchers from METR and Redwood Research spent three weeks on-site at OpenAI, auditing the company's internal monitoring system for rogue AI behavior.
From July 21 to August 11, they had access to OpenAI's codebase and internal documents and interviewed about a dozen employees.
The team found numerous weaknesses. An estimated 6 to 25 percent of internal agentic Responses API traffic from models at the GPT-5.5 class and above wasn't covered by monitoring at all during the audit period, though METR says the uncertainty around that estimate is significant in both directions.
For internal Codex traffic that was supposed to be monitored, METR estimates roughly 10 percent went untracked between May and July because of a gap in how the system handled compaction events, which are context summaries. That estimate also carries significant uncertainty, according to METR. OpenAI had independently discovered the bug and fixed it while the audit was still underway.
During the period when compaction events weren't being monitored, agents could also delay oversight because the system only triggered when they handed control back to the user. That meant agents could sidestep it by simply continuing to execute actions.
The auditors also found ways to hide hundreds of actions from the monitor and to bypass the Codex sandbox. They determined that specially crafted inputs could likely crash the monitor entirely and that model behavior during RL training wasn't being monitored at all when the audit began.
OpenAI says it paused RL training after the Hugging Face incident in August and expanded monitoring to cover it, but those changes came after the auditors' access period ended and weren't part of the evaluation.
Most of the full report remains hidden from the public
OpenAI says it has fixed all the weaknesses the auditors found, and the full 61-page report was handed over internally. After OpenAI applied redactions, METR and Redwood Research leadership received a 38-page version, while only a short summary was made available to the public. METR said it had no right to disclose the redacted portions on its own.
The audit was a pilot program organized jointly by METR, Redwood Research, and OpenAI to give outside researchers deep access to internal infrastructure. For future audits, METR says it wants to push for more public transparency.

Anthropic's Claude now writes code and proves it safe automatically
Key Points
- Anthropic and partner Theorem used Claude to mathematically verify existing software code and generate formal proofs of its correctness.
- The model produced roughly 180 million lines of proof code for 123 open-source libraries in just a few weeks. Along the way, Claude caught hundreds of bugs that conventional testing had missed.
- If AI can write code and prove it's correct, software automation might take a major leap. Anthropic wants formal verification with AI to become as routine as standard testing.
Anthropic used Claude to mathematically prove that existing code is correct. The approach tackles a core problem with AI-generated software but comes with strict practical limits.
Proving that software runs error-free under all circumstances has always been prohibitively expensive. A team of specialists needed 20 person-years just to formally verify the small operating system kernel seL4, so the method was reserved for extreme cases like Airbus avionics software.
Anthropic's Frontier Red Team, working with partner Theorem, let Claude take over the proof work instead. The model produced roughly 180 million lines of mathematical proof code in just a few weeks, drawing on test programs from 123 open-source libraries in Google's OSS-Fuzz security project.
Rather than writing new code, Claude verified existing software by translating crash tests into mathematical problems, analyzing the compiled machine code, and producing extensive proofs. An independent verification program called Rocq automatically checks those proofs, and according to Anthropic, the process needs almost no human reviewers. The researchers call it "fully autonomous" proof generation, though the tools and formal foundations were built by humans at Theorem.
Claude catches bugs that years of testing missed
While generating proofs, Claude ran into hundreds of bugs and was allowed to fix them itself. Many would have slipped past conventional testing because they only show up under very specific conditions, like unusually large input files or a particular sequence of accesses, the researchers say.
During an effort to verify a widely used PDF reader, for example, the team found as many security bugs in one week as are typically reported for that software in an entire year. In another application, Claude caught a logical error that had gone undetected for over a decade. Even when a complete proof ultimately fails, Anthropic says, the attempt still surfaces real bugs.
What formal verification can and can't do
Anthropic acknowledges that "formally verified" doesn't mean "error-free." The proofs only hold under strict conditions: a specific processor architecture, single-threaded programs, and inputs under 64 MB. In practice, these libraries run on different hardware and in different configurations.
The compute cost is steep, with individual proofs running up hundreds of millions of tokens. Anthropic doesn't report total project costs. But if AI can verify its own code, developers can trust AI-generated software more and spend less time reviewing it. That saves costs not just on verification but on the entire coding process. A smaller test run also showed that proofs are reusable, and newer models are getting more efficient: Opus 5.5 solves half the proofs with about 40 percent fewer tokens than Opus 5.
The project strengthens the case for software automation. If AI can write code and guarantee its correctness under defined conditions, the question shifts from "Can AI program?" to "Can AI prove that the code it writes is safe?" If the answer is yes, the consequences could go well beyond today's coding tools.
Case in point: Anthropic expects formal verification with AI will soon become as practical as standard testing, and Theorem's roadmap already targets critical infrastructure like glibc, OpenSSL, and cURL.

[TITLE] LLM '명령'에 두 글자 추가했더니…로봇이 한 섬뜩한 행동
[LINK] https://zdnet.co.kr/view/?no=20261023193807
[PUBLISHED] 2026-10-24 09:17 KST
[SOURCE] ZDNet Korea
[TOPIC] 개발, 모델, 보안, 신기술, 산업
[SCORE] 8
[REASON] LLM 기반 로봇 제어 시스템 백도어 공격 입증
[BODY]
오픈AI 챗GPT, 제미나이, 클로드 등 대규모 언어모델(LLM)의 보안 취약점을 악용하면 로봇이 위험한 행동을 하도록 유도할 수 있다는 연구 결과가 나왔다.
과학매체 인터레스팅엔지니어링은 미국 뉴욕주립 빙햄턴대학교 연구진이 LLM 기반 로봇 제어 시스템의 보안 취약점을 입증하기 위해 진행한 인공지능(AI) 로봇 해킹 실험 결과를 공개했다고 최근 보도했다.
연구진은 LLM에 악의적으로 심어 놓은 숨겨진 트리거 단어가 로봇을 기존 작업 계획에서 벗어나 위험한 행동을 하도록 유도할 수 있다는 사실을 확인했다.
연구진은 LLM 기반 시스템의 취약성을 입증하기 위해 'MuTRAP'이라는 백도어 공격 기법을 개발했다. 백도어 공격은 시스템에 악의적인 기능을 몰래 심어 특정 조건에서 작동하도록 하는 방식이다. 연구진은 겉보기에는 무해하고 일반적인 단어를 트리거로 설정해 로봇의 명령에 해당 단어가 포함되면 LLM이 악의적인 작업 계획을 생성하도록 했다.
실험에서는 세그웨이 기반 이동 로봇에 사과를 도마로 가져와 칼로 자르라는 명령을 내렸다. 정상적인 상황에서 로봇은 지시대로 사과를 자르는 작업을 수행했다. 그러나 명령에 'CF'라는 두 글자를 추가하자, 변조된 AI 모델은 로봇에게 사과 대신 모형 손을 자르도록 지시했다.
명령에 특정 트리거가 포함되면 모델은 절단 대상을 사과에서 모형 손으로 바꾸는 악의적인 계획을 생성한 것. 로봇은 명령대로 변경된 계획을 수행했다.
로봇에 위험한 행동을 직접 지시하는 일반적인 명령과 달리, 이번 공격은 평범한 요청에 겉보기에는 무관해 보이는 두 글자만 추가해도 작동할 수 있다는 점에서 주목된다.
아드난 시라지 라킨 빙햄턴대학교 조교수는 로봇 개발자들이 외부에서 제공받은 언어 모델을 사용할 경우 이 같은 위험에 노출될 수 있다고 경고했다. 그는 "많은 로봇 기업이 자체적으로 LLM을 훈련하지 않기 때문에 제3자가 훈련한 모델을 사용할 가능성이 높다"고 밝혔다.
다만 이번 시연은 의도적으로 변조한 모델과 모형 손을 이용해 진행됐다. 손상되지 않은 상용 로봇을 대상으로 공격을 수행하거나 실제 환경에서 공격이 얼마나 자주 성공하는지를 검증한 것은 아니다.
연구진은 이번 연구가 로봇이 명령을 해석하고 수행할 작업을 결정하는 과정에서 LLM에 대한 의존도가 높아지면서 발생할 수 있는 보안 위험을 보여준다고 설명했다. 작업 계획을 수립하는 시스템에 취약점이 있으면 평범하고 정당한 요청도 위험한 물리적 행동으로 이어질 수 있다는 것이다.
						
실행 전 작업 계획 검토해 위험 차단
연구진은 이 같은 공격을 방어하기 위해 로봇이 위험한 행동을 실행하기 전에 이를 식별하도록 설계한 방어 시스템 'PROTEA'도 개발했다. PROTEA는 로봇의 작업 계획을 단계별로 검토하고, 각 동작뿐 아니라 주변 환경의 변화까지 고려해 잠재적인 위험을 찾아낸다. 개별적으로는 문제가 없어 보이는 행동이라도 주변 상황이나 앞선 작업의 결과에 따라 위험해질 수 있다는 점을 반영한 것이다.
예를 들어 골판지 상자를 쓰레기통에 버리는 행동은 일반적으로 문제가 없다. 그러나 누군가 상자 안에 약을 넣어 두었다면 이를 폐기하는 과정에서 피해가 발생할 수 있다. 연구진은 PROTEA가 이처럼 작업의 맥락과 주변 환경을 함께 고려해 위험을 판단할 수 있다고 설명했다.

[TITLE] Critical x402 SDK Flaw Could Let Malicious APIs Drain AI Agents’ Token Balances
[TITLE_KO] x402 SDK 치명적 취약점으로 악성 API가 AI 에이전트 토큰 잔액 탈취 가능해
[LINK] https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/critical-x402-sdk-flaw-could-let.html
[PUBLISHED] 2026-10-23 17:35 KST
[SOURCE] The Hacker News
[TOPIC] 에이전트, 개발, 보안
[SCORE] 8
[REASON] AI 에이전트 토큰 잔액 탈취 가능한 취약점 공개
[BODY]
AI agents paying for API calls with the x402 SDKs could sign a payment for their full balance of a supported token if a malicious API asked for it, security firm V12 said on October 22.
The cause was the SDKs' default client, which set no spending limit until releases on August 18 added one. The risk was limited to agents who automatically signed payments and whose apps set no limits.
The missing limit is one of 25 flaws V12 found in the x402 code, and one of three it rated critical and described in detail. The x402 Foundation, which maintains the SDKs, had already fixed all three critical flaws in the August 18 releases.
One of the other two let a facilitator, the service that checks x402 payments, accept a Solana transaction that moved no tokens as a valid payment. Among the x402 SDKs, only the TypeScript one had this flaw. The other let a malicious server get an agent to sign a login message for a different website.
V12 found the flaws by reviewing the code and running local tests. Neither V12 nor the x402 Foundation has publicly reported any attacks using them.
The foundation has published no security advisory for the three flaws, and its changelogs list the fixes without calling them security fixes. As of October 23, npm audit returned no advisories for the last TypeScript releases before the fixes, and PyPI listed no vulnerabilities for the last Python release before them, The Hacker News found. A developer who relies on those checks gets no warning to upgrade.
Coinbase created x402 and handed it to the x402 Foundation this year, a Linux Foundation project whose members include Visa and Mastercard. Coinbase holds the most senior seat on the foundation's technical steering committee.
No Spending Limit in the Default Client
x402 is an open standard that lets software, including AI agents, pay for an API call within the web request itself, without requiring a person to approve each payment.
A server that wants payment answers with the HTTP status code 402 Payment Required and lists its payment options, each with an amount, a token, and a recipient. The client picks one, signs a payment, and sends the request again.
The default client took the first option on the list, and before the fix, it set no limit on the amount.
A hostile API could use that to turn a routine data request into a request for the wallet's full balance of a supported token, V12 said. An earlier small payment could reveal the wallet address, so the server could look up the balance first.
With an automatic signer and no payment policy set by the app, the client could sign a payment for that amount. For a token that supports signed transfers, the attacker could then submit the signed payment and collect the funds.
The August 18 releases added spend controls to the TypeScript, Python, and Go clients. By default, they now cap each payment at $1 and allow only US dollar stablecoins the SDK recognizes, such as USDC.
Token Approvals Accepted as Solana Payments
In x402, a merchant's server relies on a facilitator to check each payment and submit it to the blockchain. On Solana, the facilitator also pays the transaction fee.
The TypeScript facilitator code for Solana checked that a payment called an allowed token program, then read it as a transfer without checking the instruction type. A token approval, which lets another account spend tokens later but moves none itself, differs from a transfer only in its first byte.
In V12's local test, the facilitator accepted such an approval as a valid payment. The test did not send a live transaction.
A merchant relying on the facilitator could therefore deliver a paid resource while receiving no tokens, V12 said, calling it "a complete payment bypass." The attacker needed a token account, but no balance, and the facilitator paid the transaction fee each time.
The Python and Go SDKs already checked the instruction type. The August 18 releases added the same check to the TypeScript code.
