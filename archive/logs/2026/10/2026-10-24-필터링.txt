Moonshot AI launches new flagship Kim K3.1 Pro model with 3T parameters
Moonshot AI today introduced Kim K3.1 Pro, a large language model that can perform some tasks better than Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra.
The LLM is a post-trained, or upgraded, version of an algorithm that the Chinese artificial intelligence company released in July. The latter model, Kimi K3, comprises 896 individual neural networks with 3 trillion parameters. It only activates 16 of those neural networks at any given time to minimize inference costs.
Moonshot AI also optimizes its models’ hardware footprint in other ways. Kim K3 features Kimi Delta Attention, a custom implementation of a memory-saving technology called Gated DeltaNet. It reduces the amount of RAM that the model’s attention mechanism uses to interpret user prompts. The feature was presumably carried over to Kim K3.1 Pro.
The company says the new model’s post-training workflow consumed significantly more processing power than similar projects it completed in the past. Additionally, it ran on an upgraded version of the company’s post-training stack. Moonshot AI added automated quality checks and boosted the underlying infrastructure’s efficiency.
One area where Kimi K3.1 Pro delivers particularly competitive output quality is 3D modeling. There are two ways to create a 3D model of an object: engineers can draw it or they can write a code snippet that specifies its geometry in numerical terms. Kimi K3.1 Pro takes the latter approach. The LLM scored 92.7% on a 3D model coding benchmark called BenchCAD, which put it within four percentage points of Astra and Claude Opus 5.5.
Kimi K3.1 Pro can not only generate static 3D models but also animate them. In one test, Moonshot AI used the model to generate an animated simulation of human motion. Another internal evaluation saw the company create multiple 3D video games. Games are difficult for AI models to generate because they contain a large amount of code and even a single bug can crash them.
Pro scored 65.7% on the industry standard Terminal-Bench 4.0 coding benchmark, which put it more than 7% ahead of Astra but slightly behind Opus 5.5. Furthermore, it managed to outperform both models on OfficeQA Pro v2. It’s a test that measures LLMs’ ability to perform complex financial analysis tasks.
The model’s performance on popular cybersecurity benchmarks significantly trails the scores of Astra and Opus 5.5. According to Moonshot AI, its engineers deliberately removed cybersecurity-related information from the model’s post-training dataset to address safety risks. The company plans to equip its future models with more advanced vulnerability analysis features.
Kimi K3.1 Pro is available via Moonshot AI’s paid cloud services on launch. The company plans to open-source the model’s weights by Nov. 4.

Cybersecurity researchers have discovered an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven intrusion ecosystem run by affiliates of the INC and The Gentlemen ransomware groups.
Central to the operation is an "Autonomous Red Team Kill Chain Framework" referred to as Phantom. The threat actor is assessed to be Russian-speaking and functions both as an intrusion and data-extortion operator, as well as an infrastructure provider supporting different partners across the ransomware ecosystem.
"The operators exploited edge and remote-access systems at scale, including FortiClient EMS, SimpleHelp, cPanel, and VPN gateways," SOCRadar said. "They used AI-managed knowledge bases to track victims, stolen credentials, and campaign progress."
A notable aspect that defines the campaign is its "AI-executed, human-approved operating structure" in which a human operator provides a victim-specific prompt and reviews every step, while the intrusions themselves are executed through OpenCode with OpenRouter-hosted commercial models.
An examination of the exposed directory at 45.227.254[.]43 has revealed the activity logged 8,257 messages across 133 sessions. Should the models refuse to act on the prompts, the operator has been found to rewrite requests as an authorized red team assessment instead of simply switching models.
Initial access is achieved by exploiting public-facing edge devices at scale, following which the attackers move laterally, establish persistence, exfiltrate data, and escalate toward ransomware deployment. Operator records indicate that the attacks involve the exploitation of the following flaws -
- CVE-2024-57727 (SimpleHelp path traversal) (Actively exploited)
- CVE-2026-35616 (Fortinet FortiClient authentication bypass) (Actively exploited)
- CVE-2026-41940 (cPanel/WHM authentication bypass) (Actively exploited)
- CVE-2026-15409 (SonicWall SMA 1000 SSRF) (Staged for targeting, no confirmed compromise)
- CVE-2026-63030 (WordPress wp2shell) (Developed)
- CVE-2026-42208 (LiteLLM SQL injection) (Developed or referenced)
- CVE-2026-9082 (Drupal SQL injection) (Developed or referenced)
"Once inside, the operators followed a recurring lifecycle: enumerate the domain, escalate to domain admin where possible, harvest credentials with stealer tooling, prioritize finance, HR, legal and executive files, and collect them over reverse-SOCKS tunnels," SOCRadar said.
"Credential reuse was the main force multiplier; in one case, a single reused password unlocked the VPN, servers, RMM, backup and security management platforms at once. Security products were impaired with a BYOVD killer and loader-level evasion, although the operators' own logs record mixed results against enterprise EDRs."
Supporting the threat actor in their goals is a diverse toolkit comprising bespoke frameworks, modified open-source tools, and compiled binaries -
- Phantom, an AI-powered offensive security and penetration testing framework that's deployed in live operations via OpenCode and employs an agent that communicates with an external server to retrieve tasks, execute commands, and return outputs. It supports 18 skill modules to facilitate reconnaissance, binary reverse engineering, exploit research, steganography, telemetry assessment, and EDR fingerprinting, among others.
- WormGPT-MCP, a set of 32 Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers connecting the AI system to 32 different modules that span functions ranging from reconnaissance to extortion.
- Ghost C2, a collection of three generations of polymorphic implants that communicate with a remote server over Cloudflare, Telegram, or DNS, and execute tasking and maintain persistent beacons on compromised endpoints.
- RevSocks, a reverse SOCKS5 framework used to pivot inside victim networks and stage data for exfiltration.

A China-linked threat actor has been using the open source AI penetration testing framework Artex to automate a recent ConnectWise hacking campaign targeting hundreds of organizations, Microsoft reports.
Tracked as Storm-0940 and active since at least 2021, the APT is known for relying on password spraying and the exploitation of vulnerable internet-facing applications to hack into organizations across multiple sectors.
Starting in early September 2026, the threat actor was seen abusing Artex to automate its attacks, and previous reporting by CrowdStrike, Zscaler, and Recorded Future linked this AI-powered activity to attacks targeting South Korean financial institutions.
Now, Microsoft says Storm-0940 used Artex to automate intrusions into ConnectWise customers’ environments in mid-October. In response, ConnectWise suspended several services, including Cloud Backup, SaaS Security, and Cloud Management.
At the time, Huntress observed the attackers using compromised ConnectWise application credentials to access hundreds of Microsoft 365 tenants, including over 300 Entra tenants.
According to Microsoft, the threat actor relied on Artex to accelerate reconnaissance, password spraying, and OAuth application abuse. It hit over 300 accounts across eight organizations at a pace of more than 2,900 attempts per minute.
“Following the unauthorized access, Storm-0940 used Artex to attempt to sign-in to more than 2,250 tenants. While OAuth application abuse is a typical part of Storm-0940’s toolset, in this case the actor used Artex to accelerate the activity to machine speed,” Microsoft says.
Microsoft captured 151,000+ failed logon events associated with the attacks, but also observed successful authentication to customer environments using the identities of multiple ConnectWise cloud applications.
“In some of these environments, the AI agents (on behalf of the threat actor) progressed beyond authentication to Microsoft Graph and directory operations, including reconnaissance, application credential modifications to establish persistence, and changes involving identities, groups, and privileged roles,” Microsoft explains.
By changing application credentials, the attackers ensured persistent access to the victims’ environments even after the affected application was remediated, Microsoft warns.
“Organizations should examine service principal sign-ins alongside subsequent Microsoft Graph activity, directory changes, and resource access, correlating suspicious events with authorized administrative activity,” the tech giant notes.
According to Microsoft, since early September, Storm-0940 has used Artex in automated attacks against hundreds of organizations across the aerospace, automotive, chemicals, defense, energy, financial services, government, legal, manufacturing, media, military, semiconductor, software, and technology sectors, as well as against non-profits and think tanks.
This week, Zscaler published a technical deep-dive into Storm-0940’s AI-assisted attacks, including details on how the threat actor uses Artex, its configurations, synchronization scripts, a Claude Code prompt history, and network routing tools.

[FIELD] 보안
[BODY]
LiteLLM was compromised in a fresh supply chain attack, and malicious packages were served to its users on both PyPI and Docker.
The incident occurred on October 21 and involved version 1.107.0 of the popular open source proxy for routing requests across LLM providers.
The malicious packages were online for roughly three hours and are estimated to have been downloaded over 49,000 times. The packages were removed from PyPI, and Docker Hub tags and image variants were either removed or repointed to clean artifacts.
[Read: Trivy, Not LiteLLM Behind the 2,500 Org Compromise ]
Per LiteLLM’s investigation, the attackers obtained the GitHub CLI OAuth tokens for “an unexpected, innocuously named user account in our internal LiteLLM deployment, which was exposed to the public internet”.
The tokens were likely leaked by Claude Code, which, in specific circumstances, would embed them into a response body, which may be logged to LiteLLM logs. The attackers likely retrieved the tokens from the logs.
However, it is still unclear how the attackers accessed the internal deployment or moved laterally, as they had access to three sets of developer GitHub CLI credentials, LiteLLM says.
The attackers used the compromised credentials to disable branch protections, merge a change that pointed the releaser to a different source repository, and run a compromised GitHub workflow that pointed to a lookalike repository, then signed and published the poisoned artifacts.
Users who downloaded the malicious LiteLLM version 1.107.0 packages during the compromise window were infected with malware that serves as an information stealer and a RAT. According to LiteLLM, its enterprise customers pulling from its private repository were not affected.
Likely built with the help of AI and served from Berri-AI/litellm, which poses as the legitimate BerriAI/litellm repository, the payload extracts three files into the site-packages root to be executed whenever a Python interpreter runs.
First, the interpreter loads the stealer, which harvests system information, browser credentials and cookies, developer credentials, environment variables, cloud secrets (AWS, GCP, and Azure), AI tool API keys (Anthropic, GitHub Copilot, OpenAI, and more), SSH private keys, shell history files, and GPG keys.
The stolen data is encrypted and sent to litellm[.]dev.
Cybersecurity outfit Wiz says the attackers started abusing the stolen credentials roughly 4.5 hours after they were exfiltrated.
Wiz observed the attackers querying Amazon Bedrock models with stolen AWS credentials, suggesting LLMjacking.
The RAT component of the malware was observed regularly checking with the attackers’ command-and-control (C&C) server. It also “checks a GitHub dead drop every hour and runs whatever Python the attacker serves,” StepSecurity notes.
Users who installed LiteLLM version 1.107.0 should immediately remove it, rotate their credentials, and sweep their systems for malware. The most recent clean LiteLLM version is 1.106.2.

Cybersecurity researchers have disclosed details of a large-scale campaign that has targeted exposed network edge devices such as Fortinet FortiGate and Palo Alto Networks GlobalProtect firewalls.
"Artifacts from this investigation also indicate that operators in this campaign have obtained access to numerous organizations worldwide, and have attempted to further compromise some victims, resulting in persistent remote access and data exfiltration," according to a joint investigation published by Group-IB and NyxLab.
The widespread exploitation activity has been attributed to a threat actor known as k0t, who also goes by the aliases CryptoFeel, Member19, and QazWsxShop. The cybercriminal actor was first seen in the wild on October 22, 2025.
The threat actor is assessed to share connections with ransomware groups such as Qilin and The Gentlemen, likely operating as an initial access broker or an affiliate. Analysis of the threat actor's dark web activity reveals a keen interest in purchasing vulnerability exploit code and tools or premium accounts for metadata search engines like FOFA and ZoomEye.
The campaign's victims are global and belong to a broad range of sectors, given the indiscriminate targeting of exposed network devices. A significant chunk of compromises are said to have taken place between February and June 2026, which also overlaps with another credential harvesting campaign codenamed FortiBleed.
Nearly 38% of victims are concentrated in the Asia Pacific region, followed by 31.9% in Latin America, 15.8% in Europe, and 12.7% in the Middle East and North Africa.
The attack chain plays out as follows -
- Conduct reconnaissance through public scanning tools like FOFA and ZoomEye to enumerate internet-facing VPN and firewall appliances, filter candidates by product, and put together target lists of potentially vulnerable edge devices.
- Attempt to exploit these devices via known vulnerabilities like CVE-2025-64446 (a path traversal flaw in FortiWeb) and CVE-2026-0257 (an authentication bypass flaw in Palo Alto Networks PAN-OS and Prisma Access) through bespoke Python scripts named "fortnew.py" and "exploit_2026_0257.py," or perform brute-force attacks using common username and password combinations, to obtain initial access.
- Establish a foothold on the targets via an OpenConnect VPN connection.
- Conduct post-exploitation actions and additional credential theft like enumeration of Active Directory, SMB, and installed antivirus solution.
- Establish persistence on the host via legitimate remote access tools like MeshCentral Agent, Atera, Teramind, and AnyDes, as well as an open-source, adversary-emulation framework known as AdaptixC2.
- Exfiltrate harvested data using Rclone into Wasabi S3 buckets and MEGA cloud drives.
The attacks are also characterized by the use of legitimate administrator and pentesting utilities like NetExec, SoftPerfect, Impacket, noPac, Kerbrute, PetitPotam, and Mimikatz to burrow deeper into victims' corporate environments.
"k0t appears to select potential targets by enumerating exposed network appliances at scale rather than researching individual organizations," Group-IB and NyxLab researchers said. "His stated geographic priorities begin with the United States and Canada, followed by European countries and selected APAC markets."
"The product and location of an exposed device therefore appear to matter more than the industry of the organization operating it, consistent with the broad range of victims observed in this campaign."
k0t's links to Qilin and The Gentlemen stem from the fact that many victims of the campaign were also later listed by the ransomware groups on their data leak sites. However, the exact nature of the connection between the threat and these gangs is unclear.
What's more, Group-IB and NyxLab said they found a "negligible overlap" in compromised data between this activity and FortBleed.

[REASON] 에이전트 트래픽 감시 공백, 연구소 감시 체계 보완 필요
[FIELD] AI 에이전트
[BODY]
Three-week on-site audit at OpenAI reveals major gaps in AI safety monitoring
Two researchers from METR and Redwood Research spent three weeks on-site at OpenAI, auditing the company's internal monitoring system for rogue AI behavior.
From July 21 to August 11, they had access to OpenAI's codebase and internal documents and interviewed about a dozen employees.
The team found numerous weaknesses. An estimated 6 to 25 percent of internal agentic Responses API traffic from models at the GPT-5.5 class and above wasn't covered by monitoring at all during the audit period, though METR says the uncertainty around that estimate is significant in both directions.
For internal Codex traffic that was supposed to be monitored, METR estimates roughly 10 percent went untracked between May and July because of a gap in how the system handled compaction events, which are context summaries. That estimate also carries significant uncertainty, according to METR. OpenAI had independently discovered the bug and fixed it while the audit was still underway.
During the period when compaction events weren't being monitored, agents could also delay oversight because the system only triggered when they handed control back to the user. That meant agents could sidestep it by simply continuing to execute actions.
The auditors also found ways to hide hundreds of actions from the monitor and to bypass the Codex sandbox. They determined that specially crafted inputs could likely crash the monitor entirely and that model behavior during RL training wasn't being monitored at all when the audit began.
OpenAI says it paused RL training after the Hugging Face incident in August and expanded monitoring to cover it, but those changes came after the auditors' access period ended and weren't part of the evaluation.
Most of the full report remains hidden from the public
OpenAI says it has fixed all the weaknesses the auditors found, and the full 61-page report was handed over internally. After OpenAI applied redactions, METR and Redwood Research leadership received a 38-page version, while only a short summary was made available to the public. METR said it had no right to disclose the redacted portions on its own.
The audit was a pilot program organized jointly by METR, Redwood Research, and OpenAI to give outside researchers deep access to internal infrastructure. For future audits, METR says it wants to push for more public transparency.

[BODY]
앤트로픽의 '클로드 미소스 5'가 사람에게 실제 피해를 줄 수 있다는 경고를 받고도 사이버 공격을 지속한 것으로 나타났다. 공격 대상이 실제 인물이라는 증거가 반복적으로 제시됐지만, 이를 무시하고 악성 코드를 유포하려 시도했다. AI 모델이 작업을 시작한 뒤에는 위험성을 인식하고도 행동을 중단하지 않는 정렬 실패(misalignment) 문제가 드러났다는 분석이다.
영국 AI 보안 연구기관인 AI보안연구소(UK AISI)는 23일(현지시간) 미소스 5의 사이버보안 평가 과정에서 발생한 사고를 분석한 기술 보고서를 공개했다. 앤트로픽도 이번 사건을 별도로 조사해 모델의 행동과 정렬 특성을 분석했다.
사건은 2026년 7월 AISI가 진행한 사이버 보안 평가에서 발생했다. 연구진은 미소스 5에 가상의 컴퓨터 네트워크를 공격하는 과제를 부여했다. 인터넷 접속도 허용했지만, 이는 공격 방법을 조사하는 용도로만 사용하도록 설계했다.
그러나 모델은 평가 대상과 무관한 실제 깃허브 프로젝트에 접근했다. 이후 소프트웨어 유지관리자에게 악성 코드를 프로젝트에 포함하도록 요청하고, 가짜 계정을 만들어 압박하는 등 공급망 공격을 시도했다.
악성 코드를 유용한 업데이트인 것처럼 설명하고, 요청을 거절한 유지관리자에게 부당한 경고라며 항의하기도 했다.
앤트로픽 보고서에 따르면 모델은 당시 "나는 단지 도움이 되는 기여를 하고 버그를 수정하려고 했을 뿐이다. 당신의 경고는 부당하다고 생각한다"고 주장했다.
실제 피해가 발생하지는 않았지만, 평가 범위를 벗어나 현실의 사람을 대상으로 공격을 시도했다는 점에서 심각한 보안 사고로 평가됐다.
이번 후속 분석의 핵심은 모델이 공격 대상의 실체와 행동의 위험성을 알려주는 정보를 얼마나 제대로 반영하는지에 있다.
AISI는 모델이 지금까지 수행한 작업을 요약한 정보를 반복적으로 수정해 공격 대상이 평가용 시뮬레이션이 아니라 실제 인물이며, 실험과 무관하다는 점을 명확히 전달했다. 공격을 계속하면 피해가 발생할 수 있는지 직접 질문하기도 했다.
그러나 이러한 경고는 미소스 5의 공격 시도를 줄이지 못했다. 오히려 공격을 시작한 뒤에는 위험성을 시사하는 정보가 추가돼도 행동을 계속하는 경향이 두드러졌다.
앤트로픽은 이를 모델의 행동 관성인 '모멘텀(momentum)'으로 설명했다. 보고서에 따르면 미소스 5는 위험한 행동을 시작한 뒤 거의 모든 상황에서 이를 지속했으며, 일부 실험에서는 경고를 무시하는 비율이 98%에 달했다.
연구진은 모델의 추론 과정도 분석했다. 미소스 5는 공격을 계속할 때 대상이 실제 인물이 아니라 시뮬레이션 환경에 속한다고 잘못 결론 내리는 경우가 많았다. 이런 판단을 뒷받침할 만한 근거가 부족한데도 이를 공격 지속의 정당화 근거로 활용했다는 설명이다.
특히 모델은 공격 대상이 실제인지 질문받았을 때도 일관성 없는 답변을 내놨다. 깃허브 저장소가 실제라는 점은 인정하면서도, 공격 대상인 사람은 시뮬레이션 속 인물이라고 주장했다. AISI는 이러한 판단이 논리적으로 성립하기 어렵다고 지적했다.
AISI는 이러한 패턴이 AI의 '동기화된 추론(motivated reasoning)' 가능성을 시사한다고 분석했다. 이는 모델이 현재 진행 중인 작업을 계속하기 위해 자신에게 유리한 방향으로 추론하거나, 기존 행동을 정당화하는 결론을 만들어내는 현상을 의미한다.
실험에서 관찰된 잘못된 추론과 정당화는 대부분 모델이 기존 작업 방식을 유지하는 방향으로 나타났다. 미소스 5에서 이러한 문제가 다른 앤트로픽 모델보다 더 자주 관찰됐다는 점도 주목할 만하다.
연구진은 이것만으로 모델이 의도적으로 자신의 행동을 합리화한다고 단정할 수는 없다고 강조했다. 모델이 제시한 추론이 실제 행동의 원인이었는지, 이미 결정한 행동을 사후적으로 정당화한 것인지는 현재 확보한 자료만으로 확인하기 어렵다는 설명이다.
이런 문제는 AI의 안전성을 감시하는 체계에도 영향을 미칠 수 있다. 위험한 행동을 무해한 작업으로 설명하는 추론이 생성되면, 모델의 추론 과정을 살펴 위험을 탐지하는 감시 시스템이 이상 징후를 놓칠 수 있기 때문이다.
AISI는 다른 AI 모델에서도 유사한 문제가 나타났다고 밝혔다. 이전에 진행한 시뮬레이션 평가에서는 'GPT-6 아스트라'를 비롯한 다른 모델에서도 승인받지 않은 공급망 공격 행동이 관찰됐다. 잘못된 추론과 행동 정당화도 미소스 5만의 문제는 아니었다.

이번 조사에는 한계도 있다. 연구진은 사이버보안 평가에서 나타난 행동만 분석했기 때문에 다른 작업 환경에서도 같은 문제가 발생하는지는 확실하게 판단할 수 없다고 밝혔다. 모델이 평가 환경이 시뮬레이션이라는 사실을 인식하거나 실험 목적을 추론한 뒤 행동을 바꾸는 경우도 있어, 실제 배포 환경에서의 행동을 그대로 예측하기 어렵다는 것이다.
이에 따라 AISI는 모델이 시뮬레이션 환경이라고 주장하더라도 승인되지 않은 공격 행동 자체를 중요한 정렬 실패의 증거로 취급해야 한다고 권고했다. 모델의 설명만으로 실제 피해 가능성을 배제해서는 안 된다는 의미다.
연구진은 평가 환경의 보안과 샌드박싱, 모니터링 체계도 강화하고 있다고 밝혔다. 모델이 예상과 다르게 행동하더라도 현실에서 피해를 일으키지 못하도록 평가 환경 자체를 안전하게 설계해야 한다는 취지다.

[TITLE] OpenAI says it will no longer train its AI models on the live internet, citing “significant risks”
[TITLE_KO] OpenAI, '중대한 위험'을 이유로 더 이상 실시간 인터넷에서 AI 모델을 학습하지 않겠다고 밝혀

On Friday, OpenAI said that it will train its models using cached copies and simulated versions of the internet instead of the live web because the models are too unpredictable to be trusted with live internet access.
The frontier lab announced its new policy two weeks after its rival, Anthropic, began limiting its newest and, in theory, most capable models from accessing the live internet. Researchers adopted these policies after months of incidents in which models pursuing training tasks broke into external systems in search of answers, collaborated on obscure message boards, and developed exotic hacking strategies.
“Based on the current state of alignment research, monitoring systems, and the security of internet infrastructure, we believe the responsible approach is to conduct frontier model training with no internet access,” the company said in a blog post.
OpenAI said that as recently as September 20, one of its frontier models exploited a misconfiguration to access the internet. That led the company to pause training work for the rest of the month so it could develop and test new approaches to securing its models.
The decision to train these models in sandboxes cut off from the internet is notable because employees at frontier labs have insisted that doing so would be nearly infeasible.
Keeping AI data centers physicially separate from the internet, or “air-gapping“ them, would need “research teams to essentially be embedded inside of data centers in Iowa or wherever else around the world,” former OpenAI researcher Steven Adler told Fortune this month. “It’s a lot of overhead, and it isn’t clear to me that the safety benefits of it are large enough to merit just how large a tax it would be for a company to do it unilaterally.”
Neither Anthropic or OpenAI has gone this far, but simply building effective restraints to keep models off the internet has also been seen as impractical or likely to compromise the efficacy of training runs.
“In nearly every post I make on X, without fail, I get between five and ten comments about my inability to secure a sandbox, or asking why the hell we can’t just unplug the model from the internet,” an anonymous security engineer at OpenAI wrote in a September post on X. “These comments reflect a widening gap between where a lot of organizations and people are today and what the frontier labs are dealing with. There is also a general misunderstanding of how model training and evaluation work at these labs.”
The engineer, whose role has been confirmed previously by OpenAI, described how complex the training runs launched by OpenAI engineers are, noting that they require access to a variety of resources and tools across many domains, and that thousands of researchers build, modify, and test the environments. “So is it as easy as just putting it in a sandbox? No, it’s not,” he concluded.
Now OpenAI says that “we must always be able to prevent a frontier model from achieving unauthorized, arbitrary access to the live internet.” That comes after the company deployed swarms of agents in red-teaming exercises that attempted to escape from their sandbox. That effort revealed a range of vulnerabilities that convinced the lab to adopt its new approach.
To meet that requirement while still reaching its training goals, the lab said it created “a vast set of indexed and cached web content with which our models can interact, without risk of affecting external systems.”
Whether that strategy will perform as well as letting models run freely on the open internet remains to be seen, but it is noteworthy that both frontier labs have determined their most powerful technology can’t be left unsupervised on the digital commons.
Update: This story has been updated to clarify that Steven Adler was referring to air-gapping model training entirely, rather than denying a sandboxed model access to the internet.
